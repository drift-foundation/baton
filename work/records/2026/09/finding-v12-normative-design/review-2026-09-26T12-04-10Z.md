## My three findings, checked against the new bytes

**1. TOK-7 versus ART-7/ART-9 — RESOLVED, and by the stronger reading.** The
contradiction was that TOK-7 refused to authorize host writers while ART-7/ART-9
required host-side custody freeze and deletion. The new text separates the two
facts I asked it to separate: TOK-7 now requires every governed filesystem effect
— "allocation, staging, permission/normalization changes, checkpoint freeze,
copying into retained custody, deletion and reset" — to run in a token-bound
execution, and keeps host responsibility as *authorization, correlated-evidence
validation and its own control-store receipt*: "That is custody ownership, not
permission to execute a deletion/copy on the host." ART-7 closes the loop
explicitly — "The custody owner's responsibility does not require its filesystem
work to execute on the host; TOK-7 fixes that placement" — and ART-9 now says the
host "authorizes and settles deletion instead of walking/deleting the resource
itself". The maintenance container's scoping is stated too (narrow mounts, no
producer credentials, no authority database, no model invocation), which is what
keeps this from being a second general-purpose host.

**2. The reset recursion I flagged in 19.1 — RESOLVED, with a stated terminator.**
TOK-12 makes reset controlled work with its own fresh maintenance token
generation, admitted only after proven cessation, carrying the unresolved
condition forward and explicitly not clearing unrelated holds. The recursion I
was worried about is answered in the text rather than left to a reader: "not an
untracked host epilogue or an infinite chain of containers stopping containers",
with "Cleanup of its engine object is a host Docker operation" as the terminating
condition, and §19.3 confirming that Docker supervision and control-state
publication stay host duties which "do not require a maintenance container to
stop its own supervisor". A failed or expired reset follows the same revocation,
cessation and held-effect rules and "cannot authorize a replacement by declaring
itself done" — which was the shape of the defect the W270664 reviewer proved.

**3. TOK-9's scope — RESOLVED, and it now specifies renewal properly.** TOK-2
names the split I asked for: the trusted host is grant authority and enforcer, the
bound container execution is the permission holder, and its inside-container
manager "cannot issue or renew permission, delegate it to another execution, or
use container-local time to redefine expiry". TOK-9 answers the clock question
directly — the host's deadline owner "decides expiry for every holder, including
an inside-container manager using the container's token" — and adds the renewal
policy as an atomic conditional control-store decision with a durable operation
ID, expected deadline revision, same-operation replay that does not extend again,
and the competition rule stated in both directions: a stale expiry observation
cannot revoke a later committed renewal, and once expiry or revocation wins,
"renewal MUST refuse; no heartbeat, late renewal request or clock adjustment
revives the token". RUN-5 carries the same holder/enforcer split to runners, with
"a sent request is not a grant" — the distinction that matters.

## Two further checks the request named, both satisfied

**HOST-1** now serializes the two ownerships: preparation runs "under maintenance
ownership", is settled and its writer excluded before the task token is admitted,
"Conflicting maintenance and task tokens MUST NOT coexist", and "host coordination
does not make those writes host-local". Task admission rechecks the prepared
resource, so preparation is not an entitlement to a later grant.

**Section 17** adds the proof rows the new policy needs, and they test the failure
directions rather than the happy path: competing expiry/renewal serialize, a stale
expiry cannot defeat a committed renewal, governed effects run inside the
maintenance execution with host control metadata separate, and interrupted reset
is covered. **Section 19** now states selected policy rather than alternatives,
and 19.1 does the thing I most wanted from it: it names the existing
non-conformance out loud — "attaching a token to an agent container while leaving
the actual writer on the host does not conform".
