## P1a — the fenced reservation strands the PARTICIPANT, not just the Work

This is the consequence the approver is not being shown, and it is the whole
weight of open approval 1.

v11 `claim_work` enforces one-slot capacity: a participant holds exactly one
active claim across all Routes (`transitions.py:1464-1478`, "a participant
holds ONE active claim at a time — finish it, pass it on, or release it"). The
spec preserves this — invariant 2, "One participant still holds at most one
live claim globally" — and states it nowhere else.

`assignment_publication=fenced` deliberately retains Handler and
`phase=active` (§5, §12). §11 accepts that such an assignment may end
"visibly stranded". Compose the two:

> A worker whose runtime goes `uncertain` under a profile that is not
> certified for replacement is stranded fenced. That does not merely park one
> Work. It consumes the participant's single global claim slot, so that
> participant can claim **nothing anywhere** — every other Route, every other
> team's queue — until an operator intervenes by hand.

The model demonstrates the trap without naming it:
`test_uncertain_quiescence_requires_certified_isolation_policy` leaves the
Work fenced under an uncertified manager and only escapes by re-running
`release_after_cancel` through a *certified* manager. In a deployment where
the profile is simply not certified, there is no such second manager.

A second consequence of the same rule is missing from the transition table.
Neither the **Issue offer** row ("Work unclaimed/actionable, profile certified,
no nonterminal offer") nor the **Claim** row ("accepted offer, route still
permits participant") lists one-slot capacity as a precondition. So a manager
can issue an offer, mint and hand over a bearer token, have the agent accept
it, and only then have the claim refused because that participant already
holds other Work — burning a single-use token and terminating the offer
`claim-refused` for a condition that was knowable at issue time.

**Asked of the approver:** rule on the fenced cross-product knowing it can take
a participant globally offline, and say which of these is intended —
(a) accept it, with an explicit operator recovery path named in the spec;
(b) exempt fenced assignments from one-slot capacity, which means "live claim"
and "occupies the slot" stop being the same predicate and invariant 2 needs
rewording; or (c) choose the authority-native replacement gate §12 already
invites, so a fenced Work is unclaimable without a retained Handler.
**Independently of that ruling**, capacity belongs in the Issue offer and
Claim preconditions.

## P1b — a plain retry rewrites a committed integration receipt

§7 opens "Every authority mutation below is effectively-once." §4 calls
verification, review and approval "immutable receipts". §10 invariant 10 calls
verification/review/approval/integration "four separately attributable
receipts", and invariant 11 says retry "cannot repeat a generation mint, token
acceptance, runtime start, output mutation, or proposal identity".

`Authority.verify`, `.review`, `.approve` and `.integrate`
(`assignment_state_model.py:120-154`) take no `op_id`, never go through
`_replay`, and perform no compare-and-swap. Observed directly:

```text
after first integrate:      integrated
after a plain retry:        stale-target      <- committed receipt rewritten
```

The retry refuses — correctly, because `self.target` has already advanced —
but on the way out it overwrites `proposal.integration` from `integrated` to
`stale-target`. A successful integration that is merely retried now reads as a
failure. That is the exact failure mode invariant 11 exists to forbid, in the
evidence that is supposed to demonstrate invariant 11.

The three upstream gates are worse, because they do not even refuse:

```text
verify("p1","passed") -> review("p1","accepted") -> approve("p1","approved")
verify("p1","failed")      # accepted silently; receipt now reads "failed"
review still reads          "accepted"   # survives its own precondition being falsified
approve("p1","denied")      # accepted silently; then "approved" again
```

Each gate checks its predecessor only at write time and nothing is immutable
afterwards, so the four-receipt chain the spec sells as separately
attributable is, in the model, four last-write-wins fields. Route these four
through `_replay` with an `operation_id` and refuse any second write that is
not a byte-identical replay.

## P2a — `fenced` + `phase=active` contradicts a pinned v11 statement

Invariant 7 says Work phase carries only scheduler meaning, and §5 argues
`fenced` is "intentionally separate from Work phase". Formally that holds:
v11's `active` is defined as "a Handler holds it", and under `fenced` a
Handler does hold it.

But `docs/EFFECTIVE-BATON.md` states the rule with its rationale attached:

> Handler and phase are one fact seen twice — `active` means exactly that a
> handler holds it — so there is no window where the board shows work in
> progress that nobody is doing.

A fenced assignment is precisely that window: `phase=active`, a Handler
present, every worker capability revoked, and nothing executing. It also
reaches `wait`, which returns "Work you have ALREADY CLAIMED, so the same
participant can continue an active assignment episode" — so a stranded fenced
Work is re-offered to its participant on every wake as something to continue,
while no capability to continue it exists.

This is not a reason to reject the cross-product; it may still be the smallest
correct model. It is a reason the approval must be explicit that the
guarantee's *rationale* is being narrowed, and the narrowing recorded as a
dated scoped supersession in the owning document rather than left as two live
statements that both look authoritative. `AGENTS.md` requires exactly that:
"A superseding decision must explicitly mark the old text superseded."

## P2b — the one recommendation that contradicts v11 has no executable evidence

Open approval 4 recommends that any close ending a live isolated assignment
must carry and compare the full assignment identity. §7's Close row states it.
It is the only one of the four rulings that contradicts current v11 behaviour
— and it is the only scenario the model does not exercise.

`Authority` has no `close` operation at all. `end()` is used with
`disposition` values `release`, `pass`, `cancel-release` and `plan-rejected`;
no test passes `close`, and no test asserts that a close attempted without
`expect assignment` refuses while a Handler is present. §13 presents the
evidence as covering "the approved scenarios and the proposals above"; for
this proposal it does not.

Add a scenario: with a live assignment, a close that supplies no assignment
refuses; a close supplying a stale generation refuses; a close supplying the
exact assignment commits and ends it. It is a handful of lines against the
existing `end()` and it is the difference between the approver ruling on a
recommendation and ruling on a demonstration.

## P3 — offer uniqueness is per-Work in the spec and store-wide in the model

§6 says the manager "persists one nonterminal offer per Work"; §3 says the
control store is "shared by active managers in one deployment".
`Manager.offer` (`assignment_state_model.py:222`) scans the whole store:

```python
if any(o.state in {"issued", "accepted"} for o in self.store.offers.values()):
    raise Refusal("another nonterminal offer exists")
```

Confirmed by probe: a second offer for a different participant is refused. The
model carries exactly one Work, so no test can distinguish "one per Work" from
"one per deployment" — and the implementation it encodes is the wrong one. A
real shared store would refuse a legitimate offer for an unrelated Work.

Relatedly, two tests construct a **fresh** `ControlStore()` for the second
manager rather than sharing the durable one
(`test_assignment_state_model.py:90` and `:116`). That is the per-process
amnesia §1 item 3 explicitly supersedes, reintroduced in the evidence for the
superseding design. It is also unnecessary — I re-ran the successor scenario
against the shared store and it works, producing generations `(1, 2)`, because
a `claimed` offer is already terminal for the uniqueness check. Sharing the
store makes both tests strictly stronger.

## What I am NOT raising

- The `accepted`-before-expiry token boundary (open approval 2) is coherent and
  correctly evidenced by
  `test_restart_before_claim_uses_durable_accept_even_after_expiry`. The
  reasoning — the deadline governs consumption of the bearer, not later
  reconciliation of an already-consumed one — is sound and I would approve it.
- Drained v12 activation (open approval 3) is the conservative choice and
  avoids fabricating generation history. No objection.
- Exact-operation reconciliation across an immediate same-participant successor
  (§8) is correct and well evidenced;
  `test_immediate_same_participant_successor_rejects_old_generation` is the
  sharpest test in the set and it tests the right thing.
- The authority/control-store/artifact split (§3) is clean, and the model's
  refusal to let a manager turn `handler == participant` into a recovered claim
  result is faithfully implemented.
- The model's synchronous shape cannot represent a genuinely unresolved
  operation, so §9's ambiguity rows are evidenced only through their resolved
  replay. That is an honest limit of a small model, not a defect.

## Recommended disposition

Return W151 to `baton.ops` for the four rulings, as planned, with this review
attached. P1a and P2a change what ruling 1 is actually deciding and should be
read first. P2b should be settled before ruling 4 is treated as evidenced.
P1b and P3 are defects in the evidence and belong to the design's author; they
do not block rulings 2 and 3.

The contract itself I would approve in direction. Its identity model, its
centralized assignment-end helper, and its refusal to settle ambiguity by
participant equality are the three things that had to be right, and all three
are.
